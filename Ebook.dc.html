One

The number nobody shows you

Ask most people with a home loan what their EMI is and they will tell you to the rupee. Ask what interest rate they are paying and you get a pause. Ask how many months are left and almost nobody knows.

That is not carelessness. It is a consequence of how the loan is presented. Your bank sends a statement showing what you paid and what you owe. It does not show what you were promised at the start, so there is nothing to compare against. The one figure you can see every month — the EMI — is also the one figure designed not to change.

Here is why that matters. On a floating-rate loan, when the Reserve Bank raises rates, your bank has two ways to pass the increase on. It can raise your EMI, or it can keep the EMI where it is and make you pay it for longer. The second option needs no phone call, no signature and no conversation. So it is what happens, almost every time.

A twenty-year loan taken in 2021 is, for a great many people, now a twenty-seven-year loan. The EMI never moved. No letter ever said so in those words.

Nobody lied to you. The information exists, in a form you could in principle request. But nobody in the chain — the bank, the builder, the agent — earns anything by explaining it at the moment it would change what you do. This book is the explanation, at a point where you can still act on it.

Two

How your rate actually moves

Three things decide what you pay. Learn them once and the rest of this book follows easily.

The benchmark. For most loans taken after October 2019 this is the RBI's repo rate — the rate at which banks borrow from the central bank. It is public, it changes only when the Monetary Policy Committee decides, and every borrower in the country sees the same number.

The spread. This is your bank's margin on top of the benchmark, fixed for you at the moment you sign. It depends on your credit score, your income and how well you negotiated. Two neighbours with identical loans from the same bank can be on spreads a full half-point apart. Your rate is the benchmark plus your spread, and nothing else.

The reset. A change in the benchmark does not reach you the day it is announced. It reaches you on your own reset date, which falls on a cycle set at disbursal — at least every three months for repo-linked loans, typically once a year for MCLR ones. Between resets your rate is frozen, whichever way the benchmark has moved.

Worth writing down
Your rate = the benchmark today + your spread, applied at your next reset. You can negotiate the spread. You cannot influence the benchmark. Most people spend their energy on the wrong one.

This is why two people can take the same loan, from the same bank, six months apart, and end up in completely different positions. Not because one was cleverer, but because the benchmark was somewhere different when their resets landed.

Four

EBLR or MCLR, and why it isn't a detail

These two words decide how fast a rate cut reaches you, and the difference is not small.

EBLR — also called RLLR at some banks — is tied directly to the RBI repo rate and must be reset at least once a quarter. When the RBI cuts, your rate falls within three months, whether or not you ask.

MCLR is calculated by your bank from its own cost of funds. There is no published formula you can check. It resets annually, and it falls more slowly than the repo rate does, because a bank has little reason to hurry. Roughly a third of floating-rate home loans in India are still on it, mostly because they were taken before 2019 and nobody suggested switching.

You can switch from MCLR to EBLR. Banks charge a conversion fee, usually a few thousand rupees, and they are not required to offer it unprompted. In a cutting cycle, a single year on the wrong benchmark costs considerably more than the fee. In a rising cycle the calculation reverses, which is exactly why nobody can give you a blanket answer.

Do this today

Find out which one you are on. If the answer is MCLR, ask your bank two questions in writing: what the switch to EBLR costs, and what your effective rate would be the day after switching.

Both answers are things they already know. Getting them in writing costs you nothing and takes the decision out of the realm of opinion.

Five

What happens at a reset

Your reset is the only date in the year when your loan can change. Everything else is noise. Here is what to do when one arrives.

Wait for the statement covering that month, then check two numbers and only two.

The effective rate. Did it move, and by how much? Compare it against the repo rate change that preceded it. If the repo fell by 0.25% and your rate fell by less, ask why.

The remaining tenure. This is the number that quietly absorbs everything. Write it down every time. A tenure that grows while your EMI stays flat is the mechanism in chapter one, happening to you, in that month.

If your statement does not show remaining tenure — many do not — ask for it. The bank's system knows it exactly. A request by email creates a written record, which matters if you later disagree.

The thing most people miss
When rates fall, banks usually shorten the tenure and leave the EMI alone — the mirror image of what they do when rates rise. That is fine, and often the better outcome. But you can ask for the reduction as a lower EMI instead if cash flow matters more to you right now. They will not offer. You have to ask.
Six

Nine loans, five years, two very different stories

Everything so far has used one loan as its example. It would be reasonable to assume that loan was unlucky. It was not — but the full picture is more interesting than a single warning, and worth seeing before you decide what any of this means for your own loan.

That is the full cycle: a fall to 4.00% during 2020, a rise to 6.50% by early 2023, and a partial retreat to 5.25% since. Two things are worth reading off this table slowly.

The first is the split itself. Everyone who bought between 2020 and early 2022 is carrying some version of a multi-year extension. Everyone who bought at or after the 2023 peak is running ahead of schedule. This has nothing to do with how carefully anyone negotiated, how good anyone's credit score was, or how responsibly anyone paid. It is a function of a single date: the month the loan was signed.

The second is the tenure effect, visible in the November 2020 row. That loan was larger and longer than the others — twenty-five years rather than twenty — and the same rate cycle added eighteen years to it, not seven or eight. A longer tenure does not simply mean a smaller EMI. It means less room in that EMI to absorb a rate rise before the extension turns severe. The same cycle, on a shorter loan, does proportionally less damage.

None of this is a reason to panic about a loan already taken. It is a reason to know, specifically, which of these two positions your own loan is in — which the five questions in the next chapter, sent this week, will tell you.

Seven

Talking to your bank

Send this by email rather than calling. You want the answers in writing, and a written question is harder to deflect than a spoken one.

  1. 01What is my current effective rate, and what spread am I on over the benchmark?
    The spread is contractual. If new borrowers at your bank get better today, that gap is negotiable.
  2. 02What is my remaining tenure in months, as of today?
    Compare it with your sanction letter. The difference is the whole story.
  3. 03Have rate changes since disbursal been absorbed by my EMI or my tenure?
    Tenure is the default because it needs no consent. You are allowed to ask for the other.
  4. 04What would it cost to reset my spread to your current card rate?
    Most banks run a conversion fee and never mention it to existing customers.
  5. 05If I am on MCLR, what does switching to EBLR cost and what is the rate afterwards?
    Switching is a borrower's right, not a favour.

Expect the first reply to answer two of the five. Reply again, quoting the unanswered ones. Persistence is the entire technique — most people stop after the first vague response, and the bank's process is built around the assumption that they will.

Eight

The arithmetic of paying early

Prepayment is the one lever entirely in your hands. The arithmetic is simple, and it is worth understanding rather than taking on faith.

Every rupee you put in as a lump sum goes straight against the principal. It removes not just that rupee but every future interest payment that rupee would have generated for the rest of the loan. This is why the same amount saves far more in year three than in year fifteen — early on, there are more years of interest left to cancel.

When you prepay, you get a choice, and banks default to the first without asking:

Keep the EMI, shorten the term. Your monthly payment is unchanged and the loan ends sooner. This saves the most interest, by a wide margin.

Keep the term, cut the EMI. The loan ends on the original date and your monthly outgo falls. This saves less overall but frees up cash every month.

Neither is correct in the abstract. The first is better arithmetic; the second is better if a lower monthly commitment is what you actually need. State which one you want in writing when you make the payment, because the bank will otherwise choose for you.

A smaller, steadier lever: add a fixed amount to every EMI. An extra ten per cent each month, paid consistently, typically removes several years from a twenty-year loan — and unlike a lump sum, it needs no windfall.

Nine

When paying early is the wrong move

Most writing on this subject treats prepayment as an unqualified good. It is not, and the cases where it goes wrong are ordinary rather than exotic.

When it empties your emergency fund. Money put into a home loan is extremely hard to get back out. If a job loss follows the prepayment, you are asset-rich and cash-poor, still owing an EMI every month. Keep six months of expenses liquid before any of it goes to the loan.

When you carry more expensive debt. A home loan is among the cheapest borrowing available to an individual. A credit card balance or a personal loan costs several times as much. Clearing those first is not a close call.

When the tax position changes the maths. Interest and principal on a home loan carry deductions that other uses of the money do not. Whether that outweighs the interest saved depends on your income and which tax regime you are in — this is the one place in this book where an hour with an accountant is worth the fee.

When you are late in the loan. In the final years, most of each EMI is already principal. Prepaying then cancels very little future interest, because there is very little left to cancel.

None of this is an argument against prepaying. It is an argument for knowing which of these applies to you before you do it.

Ten

Reading your statement, line by line

A home loan statement is designed to confirm that your payments arrived. It is not designed to tell you how the loan is going. Once you know which lines matter, though, it will tell you a great deal.

Statements vary by bank, but almost all of them carry these, under one name or another.

The line What it is really telling you
Principal outstandingWhat you still owe. Compare it against the same month last year — the fall is the only real progress you have made.
Interest chargedThe part of your EMI that vanished. In the first years this is most of it, which is why early prepayment does so much.
Principal repaidThe part that actually reduced the debt. Watch it grow over the years; that growth is the loan turning in your favour.
Rate of interestYour effective rate this month. If it changed, a reset happened — go to chapter five.
Tenure remainingThe number this whole book is about. Many statements omit it. Ask for it in writing if yours does.
Charges or other debitsInsurance premiums, legal fees, late charges. Query anything you do not recognise in the same month it appears.

One test is worth doing once. Take your outstanding principal, multiply it by your annual rate, divide by twelve. That is roughly the interest you should have been charged this month. If the statement shows materially more, something is wrong — an unrecorded charge, a rate applied you were not told about, or an error. Ask.

Once a year, ask for this
Request a full amortisation schedule — the month-by-month table for the remaining life of the loan. Banks produce it on request and rarely volunteer it. It is the single most useful document you can hold, because it shows you the shape of the whole thing rather than one month of it.
Eleven

Why the first five years decide everything

There is a fact about loans that almost nobody is told at signing, and it changes what you should do with any spare money you have.

In the early years of a twenty-year loan, the overwhelming majority of each instalment is interest. On a typical loan at nine per cent, the first EMI is roughly four-fifths interest and one-fifth principal. It takes something like twelve years before the two halves swap over.

This has an uncomfortable consequence. Five years into a twenty-year loan, having paid a quarter of the instalments, you have typically cleared only about eleven or twelve per cent of the principal. The debt feels immovable because it very nearly is.

It also has a useful consequence. A rupee prepaid in year two cancels eighteen years of future interest on that rupee. The same rupee prepaid in year fifteen cancels five. The arithmetic of prepayment is not linear, and it is heavily front-loaded.

If you are going to prepay at all, the early years are when it does the most work. Not because the loan is larger, but because there is more future interest left to cancel.

The mirror image is also true, and it is the reason a rate rise early in a loan is so damaging. A reset in year two applies a higher rate to almost the whole principal for almost the whole term. The same reset in year sixteen barely registers. Two borrowers hit by identical rate cycles at different points in their loans end up in genuinely different places, through no decision of their own.

None of this tells you to prepay. Chapter nine still applies, and an emergency fund still comes first. But if you are weighing when to act, the answer is earlier rather than later, and the gap between the two is wider than most people assume.

Twelve

The balance transfer question

Moving your loan to another bank for a lower rate is called a balance transfer, and it is aggressively marketed. Sometimes it is the right move. Often the advertised saving does not survive contact with the costs.

Before considering one, do the cheaper thing first. Ask your own bank to reset your spread to what they offer new borrowers today. This usually costs a conversion fee of a few thousand rupees, requires no new paperwork, no new valuation and no new legal check. Banks would rather keep you than lose you, and they will frequently match a competitor if asked directly. They will almost never offer this unprompted.

If you still want to move, count all of it:

  • Processing fee at the new bank, typically a half to one per cent of the loan.
  • Fresh legal and technical valuation charges.
  • Stamp duty on the new agreement, which varies by state and is not trivial.
  • New insurance, if the new bank insists on its own policy.
  • Your own time — expect weeks, and expect to chase.

Then compare honestly. The saving is the interest difference over your remaining term, not over a fresh twenty years. This is where most advertised comparisons quietly mislead: a lower rate on a longer tenure can cost more in total while showing a smaller EMI.

The rule of thumb

A transfer tends to be worth it when the rate gap is at least half a per cent, you have more than half the term left, and the total switching cost comes back within two years of saved interest.

Below those thresholds, the effort usually outweighs the gain — and the effort is real.

One thing to insist on: keep the tenure the same when you move. New banks like to offer a longer term because it lowers the EMI and makes the deal look better. It also quietly undoes the saving you switched for.

Thirteen

Fixed, floating, and the hybrid pitch

Almost every home loan in India is floating, and most borrowers never actively chose that. It is worth understanding what the alternatives are and why they are offered.

Floating. Your rate follows a benchmark. You carry the risk of rises and get the benefit of falls. It is the cheapest option at signing, because you are absorbing the uncertainty rather than the bank.

Fixed. Your rate is locked. The bank is now carrying the risk, and it charges for that — typically one to two per cent above the floating rate on offer. Read the terms closely: many Indian "fixed" home loans are fixed only for an initial period, after which they convert to floating. A genuinely fixed twenty-year home loan is rare here.

Hybrid. Fixed for two or three years, floating after. Marketed as the best of both. In practice you pay a premium for the fixed period and then inherit whatever the cycle is doing when it ends, which is precisely the risk you paid to avoid.

The question to ask
If a fixed rate is offered, ask exactly how many years it is fixed for, what it converts to afterwards, and what the conversion is benchmarked against. If the answer to the third question is vague, the rate is not as fixed as the brochure suggests.

There is also a real asymmetry worth knowing. Fixed-rate loans frequently carry prepayment penalties, while floating-rate loans to individuals should carry none at all. If closing early is part of your plan, a fixed rate can cost you twice — once in the premium, once in the penalty.

Fourteen

What they sell you alongside the loan

The loan is the product you asked for. At the point of signing, several others will be added, usually presented as requirements rather than choices.

Loan protection insurance. A policy that clears the loan if you die. The idea is sound — a family should not inherit an EMI. The execution is often poor: the premium is frequently added to the loan itself, meaning you pay interest on your insurance for twenty years. A plain term life policy for the same cover, bought separately and paid annually, is usually markedly cheaper.

Property insurance. Reasonable, and often genuinely required. But you are not obliged to buy your bank's policy. Ask what it costs, then price the same cover independently.

Processing and administrative fees. Negotiable more often than you would think, particularly if you have a competing sanction letter in hand.

The single most useful sentence you can say at signing is: which of these is legally required, and which is optional? Ask it out loud, and ask for the answer in the document. Regulators have been clear that bundling cannot be made a condition of the loan, but the practice persists because very few borrowers ask.

Anything financed into the loan is not a one-time cost. It is that cost plus twenty years of interest on it. A thirty-thousand-rupee premium added to the principal is not thirty thousand rupees.

Fifteen

If you cannot pay

This chapter is here because the situation is common and the instinct — to go quiet — is the one thing that reliably makes it worse.

A missed EMI is not a catastrophe. Banks have processes for this, and they would rather restructure a loan than repossess a house, which is slow, expensive and bad publicity. What they cannot do is help someone they cannot reach.

Speak first, and early. The month you know a payment will be difficult, not the month after it bounces. Ask specifically about a moratorium, a tenure extension, or a temporary EMI reduction. All three exist. None will be offered to a borrower who has stopped answering the phone.

Understand the sequence. A single missed payment brings a late fee and a mark on your credit record. Repeated misses over roughly ninety days move the loan into non-performing territory, at which point formal recovery begins. That window is long enough to fix things, and it is the whole reason to act inside it.

Know what cannot happen. Recovery is governed by law and by RBI conduct rules. You are entitled to written notice, to reasonable hours of contact, and to be treated without harassment or intimidation. Agents who ignore that are in breach, and your bank's own grievance process — followed by the RBI Ombudsman — is the route, in writing, keeping copies.

Consider the options you control. Selling the property on your own terms almost always recovers more than a distress auction. Renting it out and moving somewhere cheaper is unglamorous and frequently works. Neither is failure; both are better than silence.

The one thing worth remembering

Every option in this chapter gets narrower with time. A borrower who calls in month one has several. A borrower who calls in month twelve has very few. Nothing else in this book is as time-sensitive as this.

Sixteen

A ten-minute routine, four times a year

Everything in this book reduces to a short habit. Do this on your reset months and you will never again be surprised by your own loan.

  1. Open the statement for the reset month.
  2. Write down the effective rate and the remaining tenure. Keep them in one place — a note on your phone is enough.
  3. Compare against last quarter. If the tenure grew, you now know why.
  4. Once a year, check what your bank charges new borrowers today. If your spread is worse, email them.
  5. Once a year, decide whether a prepayment makes sense — against chapter eight, not against enthusiasm.

Four entries a year. After three years you will have a record of your own loan that your bank has never once sent you, and the arguments you can make will be grounded in it.

Seventeen

What this book cannot tell you

It cannot tell you where interest rates go next. Nobody can, and anyone who claims otherwise is selling something.

It cannot tell you whether to buy now or wait, whether to prepay or invest, or which bank to use. Those depend on your income, your job security, your family, your tax position and your appetite for risk — none of which a book knows.

What it can do is remove the excuse of not knowing. The mechanism in chapter one is not complicated. It is simply never explained at the moment it matters, by anyone with a reason to explain it.

Now that you know it, the four dates a year in chapter sixteen are what stand between you and another quiet decade.

The words, plainly

Everything in this book, defined once.

AmortisationThe schedule by which a loan is repaid. Early EMIs are mostly interest; later ones are mostly principal.
Basis point (bps)One hundredth of a percent. A 25 bps cut is 0.25%.
BenchmarkThe published rate your loan is tied to. Usually the RBI repo rate.
EBLR / RLLRExternal Benchmark Lending Rate. Tied to the repo rate, resets at least quarterly.
EMIEquated Monthly Instalment. Fixed at disbursal and, on most floating loans, never changed again.
MCLRMarginal Cost of Funds based Lending Rate. Set internally by your bank, resets annually.
MPCThe RBI's Monetary Policy Committee. Meets roughly every two months and sets the repo rate.
PrincipalThe amount still owed, before interest. Prepayments go straight against it.
Repo rateThe rate at which the RBI lends to commercial banks. Public, and the same for everyone.
ResetThe date your rate is recalculated against the benchmark. Between resets it does not move.
Sanction letterThe document issued when your loan was approved. It contains your spread, benchmark and reset terms.
Spread / marginYour bank's markup over the benchmark. Fixed for you at signing, and the part you can negotiate.

About this book

Before LoanRepo, I spent close to a decade advising retail investors as a financial adviser. What brought this book into existence was not an idea. It was the same conversation, repeated for years.

A client would call, puzzled, because their loan balance was not falling the way they expected. They had done nothing wrong. They had paid every instalment on time. And they had no way of knowing that the rate cycle had quietly added years to the end of their loan, because nothing they received in the post had ever mentioned it.

Every time, the explanation took about fifteen minutes. Every time, the reaction was the same: nobody told me. And every time, the information had been sitting in public records the whole while, in a form nobody had bothered to assemble for the person it affected.

That is all this book is. The fifteen-minute explanation, written down, with the arithmetic worked out and the questions you should be asking listed in an order that gets answers.

It has no sponsor. LoanRepo earns nothing from your bank, nothing from any lender, and nothing different depending on what you decide to do. That independence is the only reason this book can say some of what it says.
